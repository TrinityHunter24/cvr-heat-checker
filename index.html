Two engines: a physics model and a learned surrogate

The physics model is the ground truth. It simulates a room hour by hour for a full year. It covers the sun on each wall and the roof, heat soaking through brick and concrete, ventilation, a student's heat, and the cold night sky. The My room tab shows its results for all 200 rooms.

The surrogate model is a fast imitation of the physics model. We ran the physics model 2,400 times on random room designs, then trained a gradient-boosted tree model to predict its answers. The surrogate needs 0.02 ms per answer instead of about 90 ms, so the What-if lab can answer every slider move instantly. The same speed would let the method screen all 24 Bhawans.

Step 1 · Training data: how the 2,400 runs were sampled

Every run is a random combination, so the model sees many mixes of position, material and climate. The range of values in training sets the limits of what the model can be trusted on.

Step 2 · The 13 features

A feature is one number describing the room. Compass directions are encoded as a sine and a cosine. North (0°) and 355° are almost the same direction, but as raw numbers they look far apart. As (sin, cos) pairs they sit next to each other, as they should.

Step 3 · Targets and algorithm

Two targets, two models. (1) Overheated hours: hours in the warm academic season (15 Mar–10 May, 1 Aug–31 Oct; 3,576 h) when the room's operative temperature is above the IMAC 90% acceptability limit. (2) Degree-hours: the same hours, each weighted by how many °C it is above the limit. Both come from the physics model.

Algorithm: histogram gradient boosting (scikit-learn HistGradientBoostingRegressor). Picture 300 small decision trees, each asking yes/no questions like "is it a top-floor room?" or "is the roof absorptance above 0.55?". Tree 1 makes a rough guess, and tree 2 learns to correct tree 1's mistakes. Tree 3 corrects what is still wrong, and so on. The final answer is a starting value plus all 300 corrections.

Step 5 · Running in the browser

Each model's 300 trees (14,100 decision nodes) are saved as a JSON file (about 430 KB for both). For a prediction, this page walks each tree from its root and goes left whenever a feature is at or below the node's threshold, until it reaches a leaf. It then adds up the 300 leaf values and the starting value. These are the same steps scikit-learn performs. We checked that the exported trees give scikit-learn's predictions to within 0.02.

Limits to state plainly

The surrogate copies the physics model, including its assumptions: synthetic weather built from IMD normals, assumed construction and window habits, and facade directions taken from satellite images. It has not yet been checked against a thermometer in a real room. The next step is a sensor pilot in March–May 2027. Outside the training ranges, the model's answers are not reliable, and the lab warns you when an input goes beyond them.
